# Presentation Guide (5–7 Minutes)

## Structure

1. **Project introduction** (30 sec) — name the project and what it does.
2. **Business problem** (30 sec) — what question was the company trying to answer?
3. **Dataset** (30 sec) — size, source, what it contains.
4. **Tools** (20 sec) — Python, NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib, Streamlit, Git/GitHub.
5. **Data cleaning** (45 sec) — what issues you found and fixed, and why it mattered.
6. **NumPy analysis** (60–90 sec) — walk through 2–3 concrete examples (e.g. Boolean masking for high-value orders, group-style aggregation for category sales).
7. **Visualizations** (45 sec) — show 2–3 charts and what each answers.
8. **Key findings** (45 sec) — your top 2–3 insights, in plain language.
9. **Business recommendations** (30 sec) — what you'd tell management to do.
10. **GitHub** (15 sec) — where the code lives.
11. **Deployment** (15 sec) — the live dashboard link.
12. **What you learned** (20 sec) — close with your honest takeaway.

## Sample Spoken Opening

"This project analyzes retail sales data for a company selling across
multiple cities in India. Management wanted to know which products,
regions, and customers were driving revenue and profit, so I built a
complete analysis pipeline in Python and NumPy, cleaned about two
thousand raw transaction records, calculated business KPIs, and turned
that into an interactive dashboard anyone on the team can use without
writing code."

## Sample Spoken Closing

"The main thing I took away from this project is how much a few lines
of NumPy — Boolean masking, grouping by category, calculating
percentiles — can replace a lot of manual work. It also gave me a full
picture of the Data Analyst workflow, from a messy CSV file all the way
to a deployed dashboard someone can actually use."

## Presentation Tips
- Show the live dashboard rather than just describing it — it's the
  strongest part of the demo.
- Have one specific number ready for each finding (e.g. "Electronics
  generated ₹1.8M, the highest of any category") — specific numbers are
  more convincing than vague statements.
- If asked a question you don't know, it's fine to say "I'd need to dig
  into the data to answer that precisely" — that's an honest, professional answer.
